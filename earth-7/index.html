Can you love something you already know is dying?

01 The inquiry: what's worth saving when you can't save everything?

By the time Earth 7 opens, the planet is already most of the way gone. The sky is full of sulfur and scattered light from decades of desperate geoengineering, the population has been falling for years, and plenty of people have already left — some for Mars, some into digital afterlives. Rosemary Stein, a scientist with little patience for other people, takes her young daughter to live in a pod at the bottom of the ocean, mostly because she's tired of the surface. Her daughter, who goes by Dylan, grows up wanting nothing more than to escape that pod and find something like an ordinary life.

She eventually does — into a desert research lab, and later into an unlikely relationship with Melanie, a woman she meets on a beach of artificial sand, who may or may not be a robot. The book never quite answers that question, and doesn't seem especially interested in doing so. What it's interested in is what people (and possibly not-quite-people) do with the time they have left on a planet that's already been given up on by almost everyone else.

Deb Olin Unferth spent years researching this book — sand, DNA preservation, extreme climates, the physics of geoengineering — then wrote something that reads less like a warning and more like a fable. Critics have called it "profound, funny, alarming," a novel that somehow finds tenderness and humor inside a genuinely bleak premise, rather than collapsing into either grief or blame.

For a DP student thinking seriously about climate futures, the inquiry here isn't "how do we stop this from happening." It's a harder, quieter question: once something huge and irreversible is already underway, what does it actually mean to keep loving it — and is that love worth anything at all?

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the story sits inside.

Grief
Loving something you already know is lost

Almost everyone in this book has, in some way, made peace with Earth's ending. What does it look like to keep loving a place, or a planet, once its future is no longer really in question?

Connection
What counts as love, and who's allowed to feel it

The novel never confirms whether Melanie is a robot, and treats the relationship as real either way. What does that ambiguity suggest about what actually makes a connection between two people (or two beings) count as love?

Legacy
Building something for people who may never exist

Rosemary spends her career preserving a genetic record of a dying planet's species, on the chance that someone, someday, might use it to rebuild. Is that kind of work meaningful even if no one is ever there to receive it?

Wonder
Finding beauty inside an ending

The book is frequently funny and strange even as it depicts a dying world. What does choosing humor and wonder, instead of despair, actually do for a story about something this serious — and for the reader living through the early chapters of the same crisis?

These tensions build slowly across Dylan's whole life — the novel takes its time letting grief, love, and something close to hope sit next to each other without resolving which one wins.

04 Theory of Knowledge connection

TOK lensInquiry connection
Scope and contextThe novel imagines Earth's ending from one small, personal vantage point rather than a systems-level one. What can a character-scale story reveal about planetary collapse that a scientific model or report cannot — and what does it necessarily leave out?
Methods and toolsUnferth researched real climate science, sand, and geoengineering for years, then wrote something that reads more like a fable than a prediction. What's gained, and what's risked, in dressing real research up as speculative fiction rather than presenting it directly?
PerspectivesThe novel never resolves whether Melanie is a robot, and treats the question as beside the point. Why might withholding that answer matter more than settling it, for what the book is trying to say about love and consciousness?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Rosemary spends her life building a genetic record of Earth's vanishing species for a "future person" who may never exist. Is that project meaningful even if no one is ever there to use it? What does your answer suggest about what makes any long-term effort worthwhile?
  • The novel never confirms whether Melanie is a robot, and treats the question as beside the point. What would it take for you to consider a relationship "real," if you couldn't be certain what — or who — was on the other side of it?
  • Several reviewers note the book finds humor and tenderness inside a genuinely bleak premise. What do you think that choice does for a reader that a purely grim telling of the same events wouldn't?

06 If this book grabbed you, try

Station Eleven
Emily St. John Mandel

Another post-collapse story more interested in what survives — art, connection, memory — than in the collapse itself.

Klara and the Sun
Kazuo Ishiguro

An artificial companion's capacity for devotion, blurring the line between programmed behavior and something that might actually be love.

The Ministry for the Future
Kim Stanley Robinson

The systems-level counterpart to Earth 7's intimate focus — the same climate crisis, seen through policy and planet rather than one relationship.

07 About the author

DU

Deb Olin Unferth

Deb Olin Unferth is the author of seven books, including the novels Barn 8 and Vacation, the memoir Revolution, two story collections, and a graphic novel. Born in Chicago in 1968, she studied philosophy at the University of Colorado Boulder and took time off in 1987 to travel through Central America during the Nicaraguan revolution, an experience that later became her memoir. She earned an MFA in fiction from Syracuse University and has received a Guggenheim Fellowship and multiple Pushcart Prizes. She is a professor at the University of Texas at Austin, where she teaches for the Michener Center for Writers and directs Pen City Writers, a creative-writing program at a south Texas penitentiary. She spent years researching Earth 7, including trips to the Sahara Desert and the Arctic archipelago of Svalbard to see glaciers firsthand.
