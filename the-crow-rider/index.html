01 The inquiry: can you convince people who don't trust you to fight for you anyway?

By the time The Crow Rider opens, Thia has already survived the worst of it — the invasion, the forced engagement, the months she couldn't get out of bed. She's free, she has Res, the one crow her people have left, and she has a plan: stop Queen Razel before Illucia destroys anyone else. What she doesn't have is an army.

So the real work of this book isn't a battle. It's a negotiation. Thia has to travel from court to court, asking kingdoms with their own histories, their own grudges, and their own reasons to stay out of someone else's war, to risk their soldiers for a cause that isn't theirs. Nobody owes her anything, and a good argument only gets her so far.

Underneath that political problem sits a personal one. Res, the crow whose strength Thia is counting on to win this war, is starting to lose control of his own magic — the very thing that's supposed to protect everyone might be the thing that hurts them. Thia has to decide how much to rely on a bond she can't fully predict, at the exact moment everyone is relying on her.

For a reader thinking about how coalitions, treaties, or even group projects actually get built, this is a useful case study: what does it actually take to get people who don't trust each other, or you, to commit to the same fight?

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the story sits inside.

Diplomacy
Asking strangers to bleed for your cause

Thia needs allies who have no obligation to help her and plenty of history that argues against it. What actually convinces a neutral party to take on someone else's fight — shared danger, shared advantage, or something else entirely?

Trust
The enemy you can't fully let go of

Prince Ericen remains the one person tangled up in everything Thia has lost, and everything she still feels. Can someone who belongs to the side that destroyed your world ever become someone you're allowed to trust?

Control
When your greatest strength turns unpredictable

Res's power grows just as it becomes harder to control, putting the people Thia loves most at risk from the very thing meant to save them. How do you keep depending on a strength you can no longer fully trust?

Mental health
Leading without being "fixed" first

Thia's depression doesn't disappear once she escapes Illucia — it's something she keeps managing while also being asked to lead a rebellion. What does the book suggest about waiting to be fully healed before you're allowed to act?

None of these tensions get resolved quickly — the book spends real time on the diplomacy and the doubt before it ever gets to the battlefield.

Scope and contextDoes an invented world of kingdoms and magic let readers engage more honestly with real coalition-building and wartime diplomacy, or does the fantasy framing let the harder historical specifics off the hook?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Thia has to convince kingdoms with their own grievances against Rhodaire to risk their own soldiers for her cause. Think of a real historical alliance formed under similar pressure. What held it together, and what nearly broke it?
  • Res becomes more powerful and less predictable at the same time, and Thia has to decide how much to keep relying on him. Where's the line between trusting a partner, or a tool, whose full capabilities you don't yet understand, and being reckless with something you can't control?
  • The book treats Thia's recovery from depression as ongoing rather than something resolved before book two begins. What does the story suggest about leadership that has to coexist with a condition that isn't cured, instead of waiting for it to be fixed first?

07 About the author

KJ

Kalyn Josephson

Kalyn Josephson grew up on California's central coast and studied biology and English (creative writing) at Santa Clara University. She works as a technical writer and lives in the San Francisco Bay Area with her two cats. The Storm Crow, her debut novel, grew out of a news story she read about a girl who fed her neighborhood crows in exchange for small gifts. The Crow Rider closes out that duology; she has since gone on to write the Ravenfall series and other YA and middle-grade fantasy titles.
