01 The inquiry: do we even want new music anymore?

David Rowell spent nearly twenty-five years as an editor at The Washington Post Magazine, and he opens this book with an uncomfortable observation: popular music from the last century still dominates the radio, the concert circuit, and the soundtrack of everyday life, while genuinely new music struggles to break through at all. Tribute bands regularly outsell the artists they're imitating. Bands from the 1970s and 80s still headline festivals. And, increasingly, dead musicians are literally returning to the stage as holograms, "touring" alongside the living members of their old bands.

Rowell doesn't think today's music is worse than what came before it. His argument is stranger and more specific: that a "perfect storm" of streaming economics, algorithmic curation, and plain old nostalgia has made audiences less willing to seek out anything new, and made it nearly impossible for new artists to get paid enough to keep trying. He backs this up with reporting most critics don't bother with — interviews with musicians from Yes, Heart, and Journey, on-the-ground visits to tribute-band shows and hologram concerts, and a clear-eyed look at how platforms like Spotify actually divide up the money.

What keeps the book from being just a grumpy critique is Rowell's own admitted ambivalence. He isn't anti-technology or anti-nostalgia — he's a lifelong music fan working through his own complicated feelings about hologram tours and algorithmic playlists, which makes his argument harder to dismiss as simple old-guard grumbling.

For a student thinking about culture, technology, or how economic systems shape what gets made and heard, this book is a compact case study in how something as ordinary as "what's on the radio" can reveal much bigger forces at work underneath it.

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the book sits inside.

Nostalgia
Comfort in the familiar

Rowell argues that many listeners have quietly stopped looking for new music, content with songs they already know. What do you think pulls people toward the familiar rather than the new, in music or anywhere else?

Economics
Who actually gets paid?

Streaming platforms pay artists a small fraction of a cent per play, a system the book argues has reshaped what music gets made in the first place. Whose responsibility is it to fix that — listeners, platforms, artists, or someone else?

Technology
Bringing back the dead

Hologram tours let deceased musicians keep "performing" for paying audiences. Is there a meaningful difference between enjoying a recording of someone who has died and paying to watch a hologram of them appear to perform live?

Culture
A shrinking shared soundtrack

Rowell suggests earlier generations shared more music in common than audiences do today, even as more music than ever is technically available. What do you think gets lost when a culture's shared references start to fragment?

These tensions build across memoir, reporting, and industry history — the book takes its time letting each piece of evidence complicate Rowell's own initial hunch about why new music struggles to break through.

04 Theory of Knowledge connection

TOK lensInquiry connection
Scope and contextRowell draws on memoir, reporting, and industry data to build his argument. Can a personal, opinionated account like this one still count as a credible way of understanding a large-scale cultural shift, or does it need to be checked against harder data?
Methods and toolsThe book leans on interviews with musicians and industry insiders rather than large-scale listener surveys. What does that method reveal that survey data might miss, and what might it miss that data would catch?
PerspectivesRowell writes primarily as a critic and longtime fan, not from inside the streaming platforms or record labels he critiques. How does that vantage point shape which parts of this story he's best positioned to tell — and which parts he isn't?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Rowell argues that many listeners have quietly stopped looking for new music, content with what they already know. Think about your own listening habits. How much of what you listen to is new versus familiar, and why do you think that balance exists?
  • The book describes hologram tours of dead musicians as commercially successful but ethically murky. Do you think there's a meaningful difference between enjoying a recording of a musician who has died and paying to see a hologram of them "perform" live?
  • Streaming platforms pay artists a small fraction of a cent per play, a system the book argues has reshaped what music gets made. Whose responsibility do you think it is to fix that, and why?

06 If this book grabbed you, try

How Music Works
David Byrne

A musician's own account of how technology, venues, and economics have always shaped what music gets made and how it reaches listeners.

Mood Machine
Liz Pelly

A sharper, more investigative look specifically at Spotify's business model and the costs it has quietly passed on to musicians and listeners alike.

Perfecting Sound Forever
Greg Milner

A history of recording technology itself, tracing how the tools used to capture music have always quietly shaped what music sounds like.

07 About the author

DR

David Rowell

David Rowell grew up in North Carolina and graduated from the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. For nearly twenty-five years he was an editor at The Washington Post Magazine, and he has taught literary journalism in the MFA program at American University. He is currently a senior editor at the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists. His previous books include the novel The Train of Small Mercies and Wherever the Sound Takes You: Heroics and Heartbreak in Music Making. He lives just outside Chapel Hill, North Carolina.
