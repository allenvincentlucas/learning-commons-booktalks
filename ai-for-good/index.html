01 The inquiry: what does AI actually do once it leaves the pitch deck?

Most conversations about artificial intelligence pull toward one of two extremes: AI will solve everything, or AI will end everything. Josh Tyrangiel, a veteran journalist who has run newsrooms at TIME, Bloomberg, and Vice, spent years covering both versions of that story before deciding neither one was true — or at least, neither one was useful. So instead of interviewing the handful of executives building the largest AI models, he went looking for the people actually trying to use the technology to solve a specific, unglamorous problem: doctors at the Cleveland Clinic trying to catch sepsis earlier, teachers in an Indiana school district experimenting with an AI tutor, engineers inside the IRS and a Pentagon war room trying to make a slow-moving bureaucracy slightly less slow.

What he finds isn't a clean success story. Some of it works. Some of it stalls, gets rebuilt, or produces results nobody can quite take credit for — the Cleveland Clinic's sepsis-detection algorithm coincides with fewer deaths, but Tyrangiel is honest that it's genuinely hard to separate the algorithm's contribution from the fact that staff were simply paying closer attention because they knew the software was watching too.

That honesty is the real subject of the book: AI's actual impact on the world isn't determined by how powerful the underlying model is. It's determined by whether the humans and institutions around it — their incentives, their culture, their willingness to stay involved rather than hand off the decision entirely — are set up to use it well.

For a student who has to think seriously about AI's role in their own school, future workplace, or society, this book offers something rarer than optimism or alarm: a method for actually evaluating a specific claim about what a specific tool did, rather than a verdict on "AI" as one single thing.

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the book sits inside.

Evidence
Judging AI by what it actually does

Tyrangiel keeps returning to a hard question: how do you know if an AI tool is actually responsible for a good outcome, or if something else (attention, awareness, coincidence) explains it? What would it take to actually prove a claim like that — and how often does anyone bother to check?

Human oversight
Whose judgment stays in the loop?

The book's clearest success stories involve AI that augments a professional's judgment rather than replacing it — a doctor who still makes the call, a teacher who still decides what a student needs. What happens to accountability once a system is designed to remove the human from that loop entirely?

Institutions
Why good tools fail inside messy systems

Khanmigo's first version underwhelmed despite genuine enthusiasm and real investment — the technology wasn't the only variable. Incentives, culture, and how a tool is actually woven into people's existing routines turn out to matter as much as how sophisticated the AI is. Why does that get so little attention in most AI coverage?

Optimism
Cautious optimism as a discipline

Tyrangiel deliberately avoids both hype and doom, choosing a harder, more qualified kind of hope instead. Is a measured, evidence-based middle position actually more trustworthy than the louder narratives on either side of it — or does it just make for a less compelling story?

These tensions build case by case, institution by institution — the book resists a single tidy conclusion about AI in favor of showing its readers how to weigh the evidence for themselves.

04 Theory of Knowledge connection

TOK lensInquiry connection
Scope and contextCan a book built from a handful of detailed case studies support a general claim about "how AI works," or does it only tell us about these specific deployments?
Methods and toolsTyrangiel relies on embedded, on-the-ground reporting rather than aggregate data or controlled studies. What does that method reveal that statistics alone might miss — and what might it miss that statistics would catch?
PerspectivesThe book centers educators, doctors, and civil servants rather than the executives building the underlying AI models. How does whose story gets told change what we come to believe is true about a technology?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Tyrangiel argues AI succeeds when it augments human judgment rather than replacing it, illustrated by the Cleveland Clinic's sepsis-detection tool. Where in your own school or life might an AI tool genuinely add support without taking over a decision that should stay human — and where would replacing that judgment instead of assisting it cross a line?
  • The book's account of Khanmigo describes strong enthusiasm from adults early on but more tepid results from the students actually using it. Why might the people making decisions about a new technology often be its least reliable judges of whether it's actually working?
  • Reviewers have noted this book takes a deliberately different lane than either "AI will save us" or "AI will end us" narratives. Why might a measured middle position be harder to write, market, or believe than the louder narratives on either side of it — and does that make it more or less trustworthy?

06 If this book grabbed you, try

Weapons of Math Destruction
Cathy O'Neil

The flip side of Tyrangiel's case studies — a close look at what happens when algorithms deployed inside real institutions go wrong instead of right.

The Alignment Problem
Brian Christian

Digs deeper into exactly the "keeping humans in the loop" question this book raises, tracing what happens when AI systems drift from what people actually want them to do.

Prediction Machines
Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans & Avi Goldfarb

A more economics-grounded look at how AI actually gets adopted inside organizations — the same "incentives and institutions matter more than the model" argument, from a different angle.

07 About the author

JT

Josh Tyrangiel

Josh Tyrangiel is a journalist and documentary producer who has led newsrooms at TIME, Bloomberg Businessweek, and Vice News, and now writes for The Atlantic. A twelve-time Emmy and Peabody Award-winning producer, he created Vice News Tonight on HBO and has produced feature-length documentaries for HBO, Netflix, and Apple TV. As editor of Bloomberg Businessweek and later chief content officer of Bloomberg Media, he was named Advertising Age's Editor of the Year for his work revitalizing the publication. AI for Good grew out of a column on artificial intelligence he wrote for The Washington Post, and drew on reporting trips inside institutions including the Cleveland Clinic, the IRS, and a Pentagon war room.
