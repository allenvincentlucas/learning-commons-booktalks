01 The inquiry: what are you willing to inherit?

In an alternate history where the Homestead Act extended into the far Arctic, a young Moose Bloomer arrives with a wagon train of settlers determined to "civilize the Glacial Front." Generations later, his descendants — the Spahr family — live in Jubilation House, a home on stilts over a fjord, farming octopus and sea crops just to get by as glaciers melt and the water keeps rising around them. Then a neighbor dredges up a centuries-old piano, perfectly preserved in the frigid water, and everything changes: piano hunting becomes the territory's new gold rush, and the Spahrs throw themselves into it, chasing a payout that could finally save their sinking home.

Told from a rotating cast of narrators — Milda, Finley, and Temperance in the present, their parents Viola and Fry, and Moose himself generations back — the novel keeps circling the same question from different angles: what do people owe the choices their ancestors made for them, and what does it cost to keep dragging that inheritance forward?

Kirkus called it "wondrously inventive," and reviewers have compared its blend of frontier history and climate anxiety to Karen Russell's Swamplandia! and Daniel Mason's North Woods — but it's very much its own strange, tender thing, funny and heartbreaking often in the same paragraph.

For a student thinking about inheritance, climate migration, or what a family owes to the place that raised it, the Spahrs' story asks a harder question than "should we stay or go": what happens to the things — and the dreams — that get left behind when a family finally has to choose?

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the story sits inside.

Inheritance
What are you willing to inherit?

The Spahrs inherit land, debts, and an entire piano-hunting economy their ancestors built without ever asking them. What do you think a family actually owes to the choices made by the generations before it?

Home
Loving a place that keeps trying to sink you

Jubilation House is quite literally going underwater, and the Spahrs stay anyway. What do you think actually makes a place worth holding onto, even as it becomes harder and harder to live in?

Ambition
When a lucky find becomes an obsession

The piano-hunting boom promises to save the family financially, but it also threatens to consume them. Where's the line between a reasonable pursuit of opportunity and something that starts costing more than it could ever pay back?

Legacy
The strange things we carry into the future

The pianos were worthless junk to the settlers who abandoned them, and priceless treasure to their descendants generations later. What does that shift suggest about how value actually gets assigned to the things we inherit?

These tensions build across generations — the novel takes its time letting the reader see both how a family's founding choices get made, and what it costs their descendants to live inside those choices.

04 Theory of Knowledge connection

TOK lensInquiry connection
Scope and contextThe novel is set in an alternate history where the Homestead Act extended into the Arctic. What does inventing an alternate past let a writer say about climate change and westward expansion that a straightforwardly realistic novel couldn't?
Methods and toolsThe story is told from many generations' perspectives, jumping between ancestors and descendants. What does that structure reveal about inherited choices that a single-generation story would miss?
PerspectivesThe pianos are worthless junk to the people who originally abandoned them, and priceless treasure to their descendants. What does that shift suggest about how value gets assigned to objects, rather than being fixed?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • The Spahr family's entire economy shifts overnight once a neighbor's abandoned piano becomes valuable. Think of something in your own life whose value seems fixed. What would have to change for that value to shift as dramatically as it does for the Spahrs?
  • The family lives in a place that's quite literally sinking beneath them, yet they refuse to leave. What do you think makes a place worth staying in even as it becomes less livable, and where would you personally draw that line?
  • The novel jumps between the ancestor who founded the settlement and his descendants generations later. What do you think Moose Bloomer would make of what his choices set in motion, and how much responsibility do ancestors bear for consequences they couldn't have predicted?

06 If this book grabbed you, try

Swamplandia!
Karen Russell

Another eccentric family running a strange business at the edge of a vanishing wilderness, told with the same blend of whimsy and heartbreak.

North Woods
Daniel Mason

A single plot of land traced across centuries and generations of very different inhabitants — the structural cousin to the Spahrs' multigenerational story.

To the Bright Edge of the World
Eowyn Ivey

Another Alaska-set story balancing real hardship against genuine wonder, from an author explicitly named as a comparison for Shaw's own writing.

07 About the author

KS

Kendra Langford Shaw

Kendra Langford Shaw holds an MFA from the University of Michigan and has held fellowships at the MacDowell Colony, Yaddo, and the Vermont Studio Center. Her short fiction has appeared in the Antioch Review, StoryQuarterly, and the Mid-American Review. Born in Ketchikan, Alaska, and raised in Sitka before her family relocated to a rural ranch in Montana, she now serves on the Billings, Montana city council, representing Ward 1. She spent more than a decade writing The Pillagers' Guide to Arctic Pianos, drawing on childhood memories of her father, a music teacher, delivering instruments by float plane to remote Alaskan bush schools.
