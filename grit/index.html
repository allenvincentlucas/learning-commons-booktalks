Why isn't talent the same thing as grit?

A BookTalk on Grit by Angela Duckworth

01 The inquiry: what actually predicts who sticks with it?

Ask almost anyone what separates the people who make it from the people who don't, and "talent" is usually near the top of the list. It's a tidy explanation — some people are just built for it, and the rest of us aren't.

Psychologist Angela Duckworth spent years testing that assumption against real data: cadets quitting West Point in their first summer, spelling-bee finalists, rookie teachers in some of the toughest classrooms in the country. What she found again and again wasn't raw ability. It was a combination of sustained passion and stubborn persistence toward a long-term goal — what she calls grit.

That reframing raises an uncomfortable question for anyone who's ever been told they "just don't have it": if talent isn't the deciding factor, what is — and can it actually be built, or is it as fixed as the traits it's supposed to replace?

For an IB student juggling CAS commitments, extended essays, and the slow grind of skills that don't improve overnight, this book is less a motivational pep talk than a genuine argument about where effort fits into the equation of achievement.

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions Duckworth's research sits inside.

Achievement
Talent vs. deliberate effort

Duckworth proposes that effort shapes outcomes twice over — once turning talent into skill, and again turning skill into achievement. Does that mean natural ability barely matters, or does it just shift where the advantage shows up?

Persistence
Passion that outlasts the spark

Starting something with enthusiasm is easy. Duckworth argues real grit shows up months or years later, once the initial excitement has worn off. What keeps someone going after the novelty disappears?

Mindset
Fixed belief vs. growth belief

Whether a setback feels like a permanent verdict or a temporary data point may depend less on the setback itself than on what someone already believes about whether ability can change at all.

Purpose
Job, career, or calling

The same task can feel like a chore, a ladder to climb, or something worth doing for its own sake, depending on how a person frames it. Duckworth asks whether that framing can be chosen, or whether it just happens to people.

None of these get a clean resolution in the book's opening chapters — Duckworth builds her case slowly, testing her own hypothesis against the same skepticism a careful reader would bring to it.

04 Theory of Knowledge connection

TOK lensInquiry connection
Scope and contextDoes "grit" mean the same thing across a spelling bee, a military academy, and a sales floor, or does the concept quietly change shape depending on the domain?
Methods and toolsDuckworth measures grit largely through self-report surveys. How much can asking people to rate their own perseverance actually tell us, compared to observing their behavior directly?
PerspectivesWhose definition of "success" underlies this research — and could a trait that predicts achievement in one culture or economic context fail to predict it in another?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Duckworth argues that effort counts twice — once to build a skill, once to make that skill productive. Pick a skill you're developing through CAS and describe what "effort counting twice" would actually look like for it.
  • Duckworth relies heavily on a self-report questionnaire to measure something as subjective as perseverance. What are the limits of asking people to rate their own grit, and how might that matter for a TOK exhibition on measurement in the human sciences?
  • If your passion for a long-term goal fades, is switching to something else a failure of grit — or a legitimate response to new information about yourself? Make the strongest case you can for both sides.

06 If this book grabbed you, try

Barking Up the Wrong Tree
Eric Barker

Pushes back on grit's "never quit" instinct — Barker's research on knowing when to walk away complicates Duckworth's case.

Mindset
Carol S. Dweck

Goes deep on the growth-vs-fixed belief system Duckworth leans on to explain why some people keep trying after failure.

Peak
Anders Ericsson & Robert Pool

Unpacks the mechanics behind "effort counts twice" — what deliberate practice actually requires beyond just showing up.

07 About the author

AD

Angela Duckworth

Angela Duckworth is a professor of psychology at the University of Pennsylvania and a 2013 MacArthur Fellow. She studied neurobiology at Harvard, earned a master's degree in neuroscience at Oxford, and completed her PhD in psychology at Penn. Before her research career, she taught math in public school classrooms — an experience that shaped her early questions about what separates struggling students from thriving ones. She is the founder of Character Lab, a nonprofit focused on advancing the science of character development, and has advised organizations ranging from the White House to professional sports teams. Grit was her first book.
