01 The inquiry: what does it cost to build a home nobody will fully claim as yours?

In the early 1900s, teenage Sunja falls for a wealthy stranger near her home in Korea. When she discovers she's pregnant and that he's already married, she refuses his money and instead accepts a marriage offer from a gentle, ailing minister passing through on his way to Japan. That single decision sets off a family saga that spans four generations and nearly eighty years, from Japan's colonization of Korea through World War II and into the economic boom of 1980s Tokyo.

What makes Pachinko more than a family drama is where that family ends up living: as Zainichi Koreans, ethnically Korean but born, raised, and often buried in Japan, without ever being fully accepted as Japanese. Min Jin Lee spent years researching this history and interviewing real Zainichi Koreans before writing the novel, and it shows in how precisely the book tracks the specific, grinding ways discrimination shapes a person's choices — which jobs are open to them, which names they're allowed to use, which futures are realistically available to their children.

Across four generations, nearly every character in this book makes a defining sacrifice for someone else's future — often quietly, without any recognition, and without any guarantee it will actually work. The novel doesn't resolve that tension. It simply keeps asking what it means to belong somewhere that will never quite let you belong.

For an IB student thinking about migration, identity, or the long reach of historical injustice into ordinary family life, Pachinko offers something rare: an intimate, human-scale window into nearly a century of history that most Western readers never encounter in a textbook.

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the story sits inside.

Identity
Belonging nowhere, fully

The family in this novel is ethnically Korean but lives its entire life in Japan, accepted by neither nation completely. What does it do to a person's sense of self to grow up in a country that treats you as permanently foreign, generation after generation?

History
Ordinary people living through extraordinary history

Colonization, war, and economic upheaval shape this family's choices without ever asking permission. How much of any person's life, in this novel or in reality, is actually determined by forces entirely outside their control?

Sacrifice
What one generation gives up for the next

Parents in this book routinely make impossible, costly choices so their children might have something better. Does that sacrifice actually pay off across generations, or does the book suggest each generation simply inherits a new version of the same struggle?

Secrecy
The weight of secrets kept to survive

Characters across the generations hide truths about their pasts, their work, and their parentage in order to get by in a hostile world. What does that concealment cost the people who carry it, even when it succeeds at protecting them?

These tensions build slowly across nearly eighty years — the novel takes its time letting each generation inherit, and sometimes escape, the consequences of the choices before it.

04 Theory of Knowledge connection

TOK lensInquiry connection
Scope and contextA single family's story spans nearly eighty years of history. What can a novel do with that kind of scope that a textbook or historical record cannot — and what are the risks of treating one family's fictional experience as representative of a whole diaspora?
Methods and toolsLee spent years living in Japan and interviewing real Zainichi Koreans before writing this novel. How does research-based fiction differ, as a way of knowing, from academic history or journalism covering the same community?
PerspectivesThe novel is narrated entirely from the family's perspective, never from the institutions enforcing discrimination against them. How does whose perspective anchors a story change what a reader comes to understand as true about a historical injustice?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Nearly every character in this novel makes a defining sacrifice for someone else's future, often quietly and without recognition. Think of a sacrifice made by someone in your own family's history. What did it cost them, and did it achieve what they hoped it would?
  • The Zainichi Korean characters in this book are legally, culturally, and socially treated as outsiders in the only country most of them have ever lived in. Where in the world today do similar in-between identities exist, and what does the book suggest about how people build a sense of home despite that?
  • The novel spans four generations without ever including a scene from the perspective of the institutions enforcing discrimination against the family. What does that narrative choice add to the story — and what would be gained or lost by including that perspective too?

07 About the author

ML

Min Jin Lee

Min Jin Lee was born in Seoul, South Korea, and immigrated with her family to Queens, New York, when she was seven years old. She studied history at Yale College and earned a law degree from Georgetown University, practicing corporate law in New York for two years before leaving the profession, partly due to a chronic liver condition, to write full time. From 2007 to 2011 she lived in Tokyo, where she interviewed members of the Zainichi Korean community and rewrote Pachinko to reflect their experiences more accurately. Her debut novel, Free Food for Millionaires (2007), was a national bestseller, and Pachinko (2017) was a finalist for the National Book Award and was later adapted into a television series for Apple TV+. She has taught fiction and essay writing at Amherst College and lives in New York City.
