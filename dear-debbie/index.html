01 The inquiry: what happens when the system won't deliver justice?

Debbie Mullen spends her working life telling other women what to do. As the writer of a syndicated advice column, she's heard from readers across New England who are dismissed, belittled, or worse by the people who are supposed to love them — and she's spent years handing out calm, reasonable, patient guidance. Then, in the space of a single terrible day, her job disappears, her daughters start acting like strangers, and a tracking app reveals that her husband is keeping secrets of his own. Debbie realizes that the advice she gives everyone else — be patient, be the bigger person, work within the system — has never once worked for her.

What follows isn't a story about someone snapping into chaos. It's a story about someone deciding, deliberately, that reasonableness has failed her, and that she's going to solve her problems a different way. Critics have called Debbie a "gleefully sadistic" antihero and the book itself "diabolically fun" — praise that sits uneasily next to the fact that the underlying grievances (being ignored, being underestimated, being betrayed) are ones plenty of readers recognize as real.

That tension is the real inquiry: fiction can make injustice satisfying to watch get punished, in a way the real world rarely allows. But what does it cost a reader to enjoy that? Does a story get to skip past the actual, unglamorous work of seeking justice — through institutions, through slow and often disappointing channels — just because watching someone bypass all of it is more fun?

For a student who's ever been told to "be the bigger person" and quietly wondered whether that advice serves them or just keeps things convenient for everyone else, Debbie's story is worth sitting with — not because it offers an answer, but because it refuses to let the question stay comfortable.

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the story sits inside.

Justice
When the system doesn't deliver

Debbie's column exists because so many of her readers have nowhere else reliable to turn. When her own life falls apart, she doesn't call the police or a lawyer — she builds her own solution. What does it mean that the book's most satisfying moments are the ones that happen entirely outside any official process?

Appearances
The performance of the perfect suburban life

A prize-winning garden, a photo shoot for a magazine, a tidy family — Debbie's public image is immaculate right up until it isn't. How much of anyone's outward life, the book asks, is actually a performance for an audience that's never invited to see the rest?

Voice
Who gets believed, and who doesn't

As "Dear Debbie," she's a trusted authority — readers write to her because her advice carries weight. But her authority comes entirely from a platform, not from anyone actually knowing her. What happens when the person everyone trusts for guidance is the one person nobody is checking in on?

Morality
Rooting for someone who crosses a line

The book wants readers cheering for Debbie even as her methods get more extreme. Where, exactly, does a reader's sympathy start to feel uncomfortable — and does the story ever ask you to notice that discomfort, or just enjoy the ride?

These tensions build slowly, through alternating perspectives and a story that reveals Debbie's past in careful pieces — the book takes its time letting the reader's sympathies get complicated before it asks you to fully take a side.

Scope and contextCan a story that treats domestic abuse and vigilante justice as gleefully entertaining still meaningfully engage with those real harms, or does the entertainment value undercut how seriously we take them?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Debbie spends years telling other women what to do about problems she hasn't solved in her own life. What does the gap between the advice she gives and the choices she eventually makes suggest about the limits of "good advice" when someone feels they've run out of legitimate options?
  • Reviewers have described this book as "gleefully sadistic" and "diabolically fun" even though its plot is built on real harms like betrayal and abuse. What responsibility, if any, does dark comedy carry when it turns serious harm into entertainment — and does that change how you think about justice outside the story?
  • McFadden has said in interviews that the novel comes from how rare it is to get real justice, and from imagining what happens when someone finally "just gets mad." Think of a situation — in a family, a workplace, or a legal system — where people commonly feel that formal channels fail them. What would a realistic, non-thriller response to that same failure actually look like?

07 About the author

FM

Freida McFadden

Freida McFadden is the pen name of Sara Cohen, a physician near Boston specializing in brain injuries. She studied mathematics at Harvard University and attended medical school at Stony Brook University before publishing her debut novel, The Devil Wears Scrubs, drawn from her own experience as a medical intern. She writes under a pseudonym to keep her medical career separate from her books — a separation she maintained for over a decade before revealing her real name in April 2026. She's best known for The Housemaid (2022), a #1 New York Times bestseller adapted into a 2025 film starring Amanda Seyfried and Sydney Sweeney, and has since become one of the top-selling thriller writers in the country.
