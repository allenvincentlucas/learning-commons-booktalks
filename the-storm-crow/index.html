01 The inquiry: what happens after your world is destroyed?

Most fantasy stories start with a hero setting out to save the kingdom. The Storm Crow starts after the saving has already failed. Princess Anthia's kingdom of Rhodaire ran on the magic of elemental crows — crows that healed, that sparked fire, that called storms. In one night, an invading empire burns them all, along with Thia's mother and mentor.

The book doesn't skip past what that actually does to a person. Thia doesn't leap into action; she spends months unable to get out of bed. And when her sister, now queen, is forced to broker peace by marrying Thia off to the enemy prince, Thia isn't handed a clean path to heroism — she's handed a political trade she never agreed to, in a court built by the people who destroyed everything she loved.

That's the real question underneath the crows and the kingdoms: when your grief, your politics, and your survival all point in different directions, which one do you follow first?

For a reader used to stories where the protagonist is ready for battle from page one, this book asks something harder — what do you do with a fight you didn't choose, in a body and a mind that aren't ready for it yet?

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the story sits inside.

Politics
Marriage as diplomacy

Thia's engagement is framed by everyone around her as the only thing standing between her people and annihilation. Is consent still meaningful when the alternative is a kingdom's survival — and who gets to decide that trade-off for someone else?

Mental health
Leading while unwell

Thia is expected to be a symbol of resistance while privately unable to function some days. The book doesn't treat her depression as something a good enough plot twist can cure. What does courage look like when it has to coexist with illness instead of replacing it?

Power
Whose side is the enemy really on?

The empire's court isn't a single unified villain — it has its own hierarchies, resentments, and people who might not agree with their queen. Does understanding an enemy's internal politics make them less dangerous, or just more complicated to fight?

Loss
Rebuilding after catastrophe

An entire species that an entire culture depended on is gone in a single night. The story asks what a people do with their identity, economy, and daily life once the thing that organized all three no longer exists.

These tensions don't resolve neatly in the opening chapters — the book takes its time letting Thia's grief and the political stakes complicate each other before any rebellion gets off the ground.

04 Theory of Knowledge connection

TOK lensInquiry connection
Scope and contextCan an invented magic system still meaningfully explore real historical patterns like conquest and cultural erasure, or does the fantasy framing let the story avoid the harder specifics?
Methods and toolsWhat can fiction do to represent an internal experience like depression that a clinical description or statistic cannot?
PerspectivesThe story is told entirely from the perspective of the invaded kingdom. What would this same conflict look like narrated from inside the empire's court instead?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Thia is expected to lead a rebellion while privately struggling with depression. What does the book suggest about the relationship between resilience and struggle — are they opposites, or can they exist in the same person at the same time?
  • Thia's engagement is framed as the price of her people's survival. Think of a historical or current example where personal autonomy is traded for political stability. What did each side actually gain, and what did they lose?
  • Fantasy worlds are invented so that real tensions can be explored at a safe distance. What real-world conflict do you think this invented empire-versus-kingdom story is really getting at, and what in the book supports your reading?

07 About the author

KJ

Kalyn Josephson

Kalyn Josephson grew up on California's central coast and studied biology and English (creative writing) at Santa Clara University. She works as a technical writer and lives in the San Francisco Bay Area with her cats. The idea for The Storm Crow came from a news article about a girl who fed the crows in her neighborhood and received small gifts from them in return. It was Josephson's debut novel, followed by its duology conclusion The Crow Rider, the Ravenfall series, and other YA and middle-grade fantasy titles.
