01 The inquiry: whose version of history gets to survive?

Professor Ember Agni hasn't been on a dig in eight years. She's stuck teaching classes she doesn't want to teach, in a marriage that's quietly running out of things to say, still chasing a theory about a lost civilization that most of her colleagues have written off. She lives in a place called Commonwealth, a tightly governed society near the North Pole that believes it represents the highest point human civilization has ever reached — and that treats travel south, into a restricted region called the Continent, as both illegal and dangerous.

When a former student sends word of an extraordinary artifact recovered from the forbidden Continent, Ember sees a chance to finally prove what she's spent a career arguing: that Commonwealth's official history is incomplete, maybe even wrong. She risks her job, her marriage, and her safety to go find out for herself — and what she uncovers reshapes not just her theory, but the reader's entire understanding of where, and when, this story has actually been taking place all along.

Brooks-Dalton, the bestselling author of The Light Pirate, builds Ruins as what reviewers have called "a devilishly clever mystery" — a novel that plants its clues carefully and rewards a reader willing to pay close attention to what feels almost, but not quite, familiar about Ember's world.

For a DP student thinking about history, memory, or how societies decide which version of the past gets told, Ruins is less interested in whether Ember is right than in a harder question underneath it: who benefits when a society controls access to its own history, and what does it cost the people who go looking for the parts that were left out?

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the story sits inside.

Obsession
What ambition costs the people around you

Ember sacrifices her marriage, her reputation, and her safety chasing a theory almost nobody else believes. At what point does conviction in your own ideas become worth that kind of cost — and at what point does it become something else?

Memory
Whose version of history gets to survive?

Commonwealth restricts travel to the Continent and treats its ruins as off-limits, controlling who gets to study the past and how. Why might a society choose to control access to its own history — and what does that choice suggest about what it's afraid of finding?

Certainty
When your whole world turns out to be smaller than you thought

The novel slowly complicates everything Ember, and the reader, assumed about her world at the start. What does it take for a person — or a whole society — to actually revise a belief this foundational, once real evidence starts to contradict it?

Curiosity
The cost of being right when nobody wants you to be

Ember's theories make her unpopular with colleagues who'd rather she stop asking. What happens to a person, professionally and personally, when being correct doesn't actually make anyone glad to hear it?

These tensions build slowly and deliberately — the novel takes its time letting the reader piece together exactly what kind of world, and what kind of story, Ember is actually living inside.

04 Theory of Knowledge connection

TOK lensInquiry connection
Scope and contextCommonwealth has an official account of its own history, enforced partly by restricting who can study the past. What happens to a society's understanding of "what happened" when access to evidence itself is politically controlled?
Methods and toolsArchaeology reconstructs the past from fragments — artifacts, ruins, absence. What can this method allow historians to claim with real confidence, and where does interpretation start doing work that the evidence alone can't support?
PerspectivesThe novel slowly reveals that the narrator's own sense of "now" and "then" isn't quite what the reader initially assumes. How does what a narrator, or a whole society, believes about its place in history shape what it's capable of noticing?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Ember's mentor tells her that studying the past isn't about understanding who came before, but "measuring how far we've risen." Where do you see that same instinct at work in how your own school, community, or country talks about its history?
  • Ember risks her marriage, her career, and her safety to chase a theory almost no one else believes. At what point does conviction in your own ideas become worth that kind of cost — and at what point does it become something else entirely?
  • Commonwealth restricts travel to the Continent and treats its ruins as off-limits. Why might a society choose to control access to its own history, and what does that choice suggest about what it's afraid its people might find?

06 If this book grabbed you, try

Sea of Tranquility
Emily St. John Mandel

A mystery spanning centuries that slowly reveals how much the past and present actually have to do with each other — recommended by Brooks-Dalton herself as a companion read.

The Memory Police
Yoko Ogawa

A society that systematically controls what its citizens are permitted to remember, taken even further than Commonwealth's restrictions on its own history.

The Light Pirate
Lily Brooks-Dalton

Brooks-Dalton's earlier climate novel, following one family across generations of a slowly transformed Florida — the same patient, character-first approach to a changed world.

07 About the author

LB

Lily Brooks-Dalton

Lily Brooks-Dalton is the national-bestselling author of The Light Pirate (2022), a runner-up for the Dayton Literary Peace Prize and a Good Morning America Book Club pick, and Good Morning, Midnight (2016), adapted into the Netflix film The Midnight Sky, directed by and starring George Clooney. Born in Vermont, she holds an MFA from Portland State University and is also the author of the memoir Motorcycles I've Loved, an Oregon Book Award finalist. She is a recipient of the PEN America L'Engle/Rahman Prize for mentorship, and her work has been translated into twenty languages.
