01 The inquiry: can ordinary people become extraordinary leaders under pressure?

Before he was a wartime president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy was a comedian who played a schoolteacher-turned-accidental-president on Ukrainian television. Jessie Asya Kanzer had a small, forgettable role in one of his films years before any of that mattered. When Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022 and the United States reportedly offered to evacuate him from Kyiv, Zelenskyy's response — "I need ammunition, not a ride" — became one of the defining lines of the war. Kanzer, a Soviet-born writer who has spent years following his story, set out to ask what exactly made that kind of response possible, and whether the same qualities are available to people who will never face anything like it.

Unlocking Your Inner Zelensky builds its case largely from Zelenskyy's public words and choices during the invasion's early, most uncertain days: his decision to stay in Kyiv, his repeated insistence that Ukrainians are "simple people" rather than exceptional ones, his ability to ask allies directly for help without groveling. Kanzer, who grew up speaking Russian and left the Soviet Union as a child, reads a specific kind of resilience into those choices — one she argues isn't unique to wartime leadership, but available, in smaller ways, to anyone facing their own high-stakes moment.

Not everyone finds the argument equally persuasive. Reviewers have noted the book's insights can feel repetitive across chapters, and Zelenskyy himself remains a genuinely contested figure — admired by many for his wartime leadership, but criticized by others over decisions made both before and during the invasion. That tension is worth sitting with rather than smoothing over.

For a DP student thinking about leadership, identity, or how a crisis reveals character, this book is less useful as a settled verdict on Zelenskyy than as a case study to test critically: which parts of the argument hold up, and which ones ask you to take the author's admiration on faith?

02 Key themes for the curious mind

No spoilers — just the tensions the book sits inside.

Ordinariness
The power of being "just a simple person"

Zelenskyy repeatedly describes himself and his fellow Ukrainians as ordinary rather than exceptional. Kanzer argues that framing is itself a source of strength. Do you agree that ordinariness can be a kind of power, or does that framing undersell what real leadership actually requires?

Courage
Choosing to stay when leaving would be easier

Zelenskyy's refusal to leave Kyiv early in the invasion became one of the defining moments of his presidency. What do you think actually separates a courageous choice from a reckless one, especially when the stakes are this high?

Belief
Faith without religion

Kanzer frames Zelenskyy's steadfastness during the war's darkest days as a kind of nonreligious faith — in himself and in the Ukrainian people. What does it take to sustain that kind of conviction when outcomes are still genuinely uncertain?

Contested legacy
Admiration versus critical distance

This book was written from outside Ukraine, largely in admiration. Zelenskyy remains a figure both praised and criticized, including by Ukrainians themselves. What changes about a book like this when you read it alongside more critical accounts, rather than on its own?

These tensions run through every chapter — the book asks you to take its central admiration largely on faith, which makes it a good text to read critically rather than simply agree with.

Two complementary videos — a full author interview, and real footage of the speech that helped inspire this book — for previewing it before you check it out.

Go deeper

Jessie Kanzer on The Hard Way Podcast. Kanzer discusses her small role in Zelenskyy's early film career, why she sees him as a "spiritual leader for our times," and what she hopes readers take from his example — useful for weighing the book's central argument in her own words.

One more idea

Zelenskyy's European Parliament address, March 2022. Real footage of the kind of wartime rhetoric the book draws its lessons from — useful for forming your own impression of Zelenskyy's public communication, rather than relying only on the book's account of it.

04 Theory of Knowledge connection

TOK lensInquiry connection
Scope and contextThe book draws lessons for everyday life from a leader's choices made under extreme wartime pressure. What can, and can't, transfer from a crisis leadership example into an ordinary person's daily decisions?
Methods and toolsKanzer builds her case largely from Zelenskyy's public statements and speeches. What are the strengths and limits of drawing conclusions about someone's character mainly from what they say publicly, rather than from their full record of decisions?
PerspectivesZelenskyy is a genuinely contested figure — admired by many, criticized by others, including over decisions made both before and during the war. How does knowing that shape how you'd want to read a book built almost entirely around admiration for him?

05 Reflect and discuss

A few prompts for a CAS reflection, an advisory circle, or a TOK exhibition proposal.

  • Kanzer's central argument is that Zelenskyy's leadership comes from seeing himself as "ordinary" rather than exceptional. Do you think ordinariness is actually a source of strength in a leader, or is that framing too simple?
  • This book was written from outside Ukraine, admiring Zelenskyy's choices from a distance. What do you think changes about a book like this when its author hasn't personally lived through the crisis she's writing about?
  • Zelenskyy remains a contested figure, praised by many and criticized by others for decisions both before and during the war. How would you want to weigh admiration for someone's public rhetoric against critical questions about their record?

06 If this book grabbed you, try

Man's Search for Meaning
Viktor Frankl

A far darker, firsthand account of finding purpose and identity under extreme crisis, from a psychiatrist who survived the Holocaust.

Leadership: In Turbulent Times
Doris Kearns Goodwin

A comparative study of how four American presidents were shaped by crisis, offering a more historically grounded way to test Kanzer's claims about leadership.

The Splendid and the Vile
Erik Larson

Another wartime leader who chose to stay and rally his people rather than retreat — Churchill during the London Blitz.

07 About the author

JK

Jessie Asya Kanzer

Jessie Asya Kanzer is a former reporter and actress, born in the Soviet Union, who emigrated with her family to Brooklyn at age eight. Early in her acting career, she had a small role in a Ukrainian romantic comedy starring a young Volodymyr Zelenskyy, years before he entered politics. She is the author of the memoir Don't Just Sit There, DO NOTHING, and her writing has appeared in The New York Times, USA Today, and The Washington Post. She lives in Dobbs Ferry, New York.
